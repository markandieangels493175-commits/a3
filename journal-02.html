FIELD NOTE 02 / MATERIAL

Vegetable-Tanned and Chrome-Tanned Leather

A practical framework for observation, small tests and documented leather craft decisions.

01

Begin with the intended use

Start with the object and the work it must do. For vegetable-tanned and chrome-tanned leather, that means looking beyond a single attractive photograph or a general rule. Place two differently tanned samples under clear, neutral light and describe what you can actually observe. Then connect those observations to structure, colour range and intended use. A useful decision is specific to the construction, the likely handling and the maker's guidance. Leather is a natural material with differences between hides and even across one hide, so a sample is evidence rather than a guarantee that every panel will behave identically. This first step keeps the process practical and prevents decorative language from replacing a real assessment.

Write a brief outcome before you begin. It might be choosing material for a daily-carry object, planning a test piece, or deciding whether care is currently needed. When the object already exists, locate its care card or ask the original maker before applying a product or changing construction. Those instructions should take priority over generic advice.

02

Observe before you alter

Good leather work begins with patient observation. Look across the surface at a shallow angle, then flex the sample gently if that is appropriate. Notice whether the finish looks uniform, whether colour lightens at a bend and whether the reverse reveals a tight or loose fibre structure. For this topic, pay particular attention to structure, colour range and intended use. Avoid turning one visual cue into a complete identification. Coatings, embossing, lighting and photography can change what the surface appears to show. A supplier specification, a known sample and a small physical test together provide better evidence than appearance alone.

Record observations in plain language: firm, supple, dry-looking, coated, open-fibred, dense, even or varied. These words are more useful than a vague label such as premium. If a change is being considered, test on an offcut from the same material. When no offcut exists, choose a discreet location and use the smallest practical amount. Stop if colour transfers unexpectedly, the surface becomes sticky, the finish lifts or the area darkens unevenly. A test does not remove every risk, but it reduces the chance of making a broad irreversible change before you understand the material response.

03

Build a small comparison

A controlled comparison makes subtle differences easier to read. Prepare two or three samples, label them and change only one variable at a time. That variable might be leather weight, stitch spacing, edge process, conditioning amount or hardware scale. Use the same tools and sequence for each sample. Photograph the results in consistent light and write a short note immediately, before memory smooths over the differences. The aim is not to create a laboratory claim. It is to build reliable workshop evidence for the particular material and object in front of you.

Include a control sample that receives no treatment or design change. It provides a reference for colour, flexibility and surface feel. If the subject involves strength, do not rely on an improvised destructive test to predict safety-critical performance. Use supplier data, established construction guidance and qualified testing where the consequences of failure matter. For ordinary craft planning, compare how the samples cut, fold, accept a stitch and recover from gentle handling. Choose the result that supports the intended function with the least unnecessary complexity, not simply the sample with the most dramatic visual change.

04

Plan the construction sequence

Sequence affects finish and repairability. List the steps from pattern preparation through cutting, edge work, temporary assembly, stitching, hardware setting and final inspection. Mark which edges become inaccessible after joining. Complete appropriate preparation before those joins, while leaving final refinement until the object can be assessed as a whole. Around a daily-carry object, consider how stacked layers affect thickness and how the seam will behave in use. A clean drawing and a paper prototype often reveal conflicts before the leather is cut.

Keep reinforcement local to the load instead of adding stiffness everywhere. Check that hardware can move without cutting into an edge and that a repairer could reach likely wear points. When hand stitching, maintain a consistent line, hole angle and tension; excessive tension can distort the edge rather than improve it. When machine stitching, match needle, thread and settings to the material and test stack. Construction advice is not universal across every leather or tool. Adapt the sequence to verified material behaviour and document what changed so the next version begins with evidence.

05

Review the finish in context

Inspect the result from the viewing distance and handling position it will have in real life. A surface that looks perfect under magnification may feel overworked in use, while a small natural mark may become visually quiet within the complete object. Check edge consistency, stitch path, hardware alignment, opening movement and balance. Move the object slowly through its normal range without overloading it. For vegetable-tanned and chrome-tanned leather, compare the finished decision with the outcome written at the start rather than judging it only by novelty or shine.

Use photographs to identify uneven lines, but remember that a photograph can exaggerate contrast and flatten texture. Handle the object with clean, dry hands and note any unexpected colour transfer, squeak, sticking or resistance. If a finish needs correction, return to a test piece first. Do not stack products in an attempt to chase a particular appearance. Multiple cleaners, conditioners or coatings can interact unpredictably. A restrained, documented process is easier to understand, repeat and repair than a finish assembled from several unrecorded treatments.

06

Care without over-treatment

Routine care should respond to evidence. Remove loose dry dust with a suitable soft cloth or brush and let damp leather dry naturally away from direct heat. Do not soak the object, accelerate drying with a heater or assume that a household cleaner is safe. Conditioning frequency cannot be set by a universal calendar because leather type, finish, climate, use and storage differ. Inspect first, follow the maker's instructions and spot-test any approved product. More product is not more protection; excess can darken a surface, attract dirt or soften structure.

Store the object clean and dry with gentle shape support and airflow. Avoid sealed plastic for long periods, overstuffing, sharp folds and prolonged direct sunlight. Keep metal hardware from pressing into adjacent leather. If mould is suspected, colour is lifting, stitching is opening, a strap anchor is moving or the object has sentimental or significant financial value, pause home treatment and consult a qualified leather conservator or repair professional. Structural repair requires judgment about hidden layers and load paths that a surface-only fix may not address.

07

Keep a useful workshop record

A short record turns one project into guidance for the next. Note the material source and description, approximate weight, finish, tools, thread, hardware, test results and care products used. Add dates and photographs under similar lighting. For this subject, include what you learned about structure, colour range and intended use. Separate observed facts from assumptions. “Edge darkened after one light coat” is more useful than “product improved the leather,” because it identifies the change without claiming a universal benefit.

Finish the record with one decision to keep and one detail to test differently. This keeps reflection connected to action. If the object is transferred to another person, provide concise care information and disclose relevant limitations rather than promising that leather will remain unchanged. Patina, abrasion and colour shift can occur with use, while poor storage or unsuitable products can cause damage. Responsible craft communication explains likely variation, invites questions and points people toward professional help when an issue falls outside routine maintenance.

08

A practical next step

Return to two differently tanned samples and choose one low-risk action: document the current condition, make a labelled sample, draw the construction stack or ask the maker a precise question. Keep the next step small enough to complete and photograph. Avoid buying a large tool or product set before a test shows that it is needed. Skill grows through repeated comparisons and careful corrections, not through the number of supplies on the bench. Use the result to update your process for a daily-carry object and retain the reference sample.

The goal is informed judgment, not certainty from a single guide. Material batches vary, workshop tools differ and every existing object carries its own history. Treat these notes as a planning framework alongside supplier specifications, maker guidance and professional advice. When in doubt, preserve the current condition, gather more information and choose the reversible step. That approach protects the material while building a clearer understanding of how design, construction, care and use meet in a real leather object.

BENCH SUMMARY

Observe. Test. Record.

Follow the maker's care guidance, spot-test unfamiliar products and ask a qualified repair professional to assess structural damage.
